a) In the helicopter lab, a linearized model is used to represent the system. Describe one
     way in which the real system is nonlinear. Reflect on how this nonlinearity can
     lead to deviations affecting the performance of the helicopter controller (you can
     refer to a particular lab exercise if necessary).
     There are many nonlinearities. These are some examples:
        • Motor saturation: The helicopter’s motors provide thrust approximately
          linear with their input voltage, but only up to their maximum thrust. Motor
          saturation is a nonlinearity, and it clearly affects helicopter performance in
          cases where the controller attempts to change elevation rate or pitch quickly.
          For instance, in the pitch controller of Lab 1, if the system poles are moved
          too far into the left half plane, the controller typically attempts to use thrust
          beyond the saturation level, leading to clear deviations and an unstable heli-
          copter.
        • Effect of pitch angle on horizontal and vertical thrust: Pitch angle
          affects the direction of the motors’ thrust. If the elevation is 0 (meaning
          the main arm of the helicopter is horizontal) and the pitch is 0, the motor
          thrust is pointed straight upwards. For a non-zero pitch, the vertical thrust
          component is modulated by the cosine of the pitch angle, and the horizontal
          component by the sine of the pitch angle. These trigonometric relations are
          nonlinear, and in the linear model they are replaced by linear approximations
          for low pitch angles.
           As the pitch angles can often reach values far from 0 in the operation of
           the helicopters, these approximations frequently become highly inaccurate,
           leading to the model misrepresenting the effect of the motor thrust both
           on elevation rate and travel rate. One effect that is often observed, is that
           when pitch deviates significantly from 0, the model’s overestimation of the lift
           provided by the motors can lead to the elevation decreasing due to insufficient
           thrust requested by the controller.
        • Effects of elevation angle: First, the elevation angle affects the vertical
          component of motor thrust similarly to the pich angle, and this effect is
          ignored through linear approximation. This nonlinearity typically has less
          visible effects than what is seen for pitch angle.
           Second, due to the construction of the helicopter, the horizontal component
           of the helicopter’s arm becomes longer for positive elevations and shorter for
           negative elevations. This affects the angular moment provided by the motors

           when controlling elevation rate, so the helicopter gets increased lift at negative
           elevations and reduced lift at positive elevations. This is most easily seen in
           the elevation rate control of Lab 2 without integral effect, where the helicopter
           tends to stabilize around an elevation of 0, and the elevation rate controller
           in practice can look like it is actually controlling the elevation.
b) The helicopter’s encoders are used in the lab exercises as representing accurate mea-
     surements (as opposed to the noisy IMU observations). Are encoder measurements
     perfectly accurate? If not, how would you statistically model the error of an encoder
     measurement?
     No, encoder measurements are in principle not perfectly accurate, as the encoders
     use a digital counter to measure motion. Each tick of the counter represents a
     certain amount of angular rotation, which limits the resolution of the measurement
     since the encoder cannot differentiate angles in between two ticks.
     To model the encoder error, we assume that for any value of the encoder, the true
     value is anywhere within the angular interval corresponding to that step of the
     encoder. We have no reason to asssume that some values are more likely than others
     within that interval, so a uniform distribution is a reasonable choice. Let’s assume
     that the measurement is valued to correspond to the center of the angular interval.
     If we denote the step size as δ and the measured value as y, the distribution of
     the true value can be modelled as a uniform distribution U (y − 2δ , y + 2δ ), and the
     distribution of the measurement error can be modelled as U (− 2δ , 2δ ).
